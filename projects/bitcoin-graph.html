Bitcoin transaction graph

Bitcoin’s ledger is public, but its users are pseudonymous and change addresses all the time. Around a set of addresses already flagged as illicit, I built and validated the pipeline that extracts their transaction neighbourhood, then tested whether graph structure alone could group addresses by owner. It could not, and showing why set the team’s next direction.

αʹThe question

Fifteen years of Bitcoin transactions are public, which makes the ledger a rare dataset for studying market manipulation and illicit flows. The obstacle is identity. Addresses are not people, wallets create a fresh address for almost every payment, and one actor can control thousands of addresses.

The full graph has hundreds of millions of addresses and billions of transactions; analysing it whole takes the kind of infrastructure only specialised companies have. So the approach was to shrink the space: start from a set of addresses the team had already identified as illicit, extract their neighbourhood, and look for structure there.

The question for the internship: can graph topology alone reveal groups of addresses that belong to one entity?

βʹThe pipeline

  1. Step 1 · SeedsIllicit addressesA set provided by the team, used as the starting point. How it was collected is outside this work.input
  2. Step 2 · Extract2-hop BFS, in parallelBreadth-first search from every seed, compiled with Numba (@njit) and spread over CPU cores.→ 31,535,968 nodes
  3. Step 3 · FilterRemove hubsDrop nodes with more than 1,000 incoming and 1,000 outgoing neighbours: exchange-like addresses that bridge unrelated communities.→ 815,984 nodes
  4. Step 4 · ConnectWCC and SCCWeakly and strongly connected components. One giant component holds nearly all the activity; analysis focuses there.→ giant component
  5. Step 5 · SpectrumNormalised LaplacianGraph made undirected; eigenvalues of the normalised Laplacian; the largest eigengap suggests the number of clusters.→ k = 2
  6. Step 6 · Clusterk-means on the embeddingNodes projected on the k smallest eigenvectors, then grouped with k-means.→ 10,557 / 26 nodes

γʹValidating the extractor

Every later result depends on the extraction being right, and parallel code can be wrong in ways that only show up once in thousands of runs. Numba was then fairly new to the team, so I treated the extractor as untrusted until proven otherwise.

Correctness. About ten hand-checkable graphs for the edge cases: empty graph, self-loops, converging paths, multiple edges between the same pair, and the directional distance rule.

Stress. Five procedurally generated families of up to 10,000 nodes, chosen to break different assumptions (table).

Determinism. A harness (tester_serial.py) ran the extractor 100,000 times in a row on each graph and compared every output with the first. Any divergence would stop the run.

In total, more than 500,000 runs, with no error and no divergence.

Why it mattered

A non-deterministic bug in the extractor would have silently changed which addresses end up in the subgraph, and every cluster built on top of it. The validation is what made a negative result trustworthy.

δʹThe result: a negative one

Technically correct, practically empty. Rather than several communities of meaningful size, the method isolated 26 nodes from one massive, densely interconnected core. Even with the hubs gone, the structure is too homogeneous for a method that only looks at global topology.

That was the useful finding. Spectral clustering sees the graph through a few eigenvectors of its Laplacian; a tight group of addresses run by one actor is a local pattern. This pointed the team towards graph neural networks, which build a representation for each node by aggregating features of its neighbourhood, and can therefore pick up exactly those local relational patterns.

Decisions and their cost

DecisionGainCost
Spectral clustering over LouvainNo assumption on cluster shape; can find small, dense groupsHeavier to compute. Louvain is faster but has a resolution limit that hides small communities, exactly the ones that matter here
Filter hubs (> 1,000 in and out)Removes artificial bridges; clustering becomes possibleLoses every direct interaction with large platforms
Treat the graph as undirectedSimple, symmetric LaplacianLoses who pays whom

εʹLimits

  • ExploratorySix weeks; the goal was a reliable environment and a first structural reading, not a detection system.
  • No ground truthThere is no list of which addresses share an owner, so clusters cannot be scored, only judged by their shape.
  • Seeds taken as givenThe illicit addresses came from the team; their selection may bias the neighbourhood.
  • Information removedHub filtering and the undirected view both discard signal that a richer model could use.
